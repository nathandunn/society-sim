    The model

    Every person is an agent with a mother, a father, skills and a cause of death. One tick is one year. Each society carries a genome of rules — marriage, mate choice, descent, residence, labour, sharing, foraging, trade, governance, norm enforcement, outgroup stance, captives, famine policy, fission size and ritual — and the rules change only what they mechanically change.

    Worlds

    • Rich forest. Forager island with a rich shore; a few clearings could be farmed if someone invents cultivation.
    • Farmland valley. Alluvial river valley; cultivation is known. Fields out-yield foraging but deplete without fallow, flood, tie villages down (shorter birth spacing, more disease) and can be owned and inherited.
    • Desert oases. Failing rains and drying oases; bands keep herds that graze, breed, die in drought and plague, and are rustled in raids. Camps must stay near water.
    • Islands. Five islands in a fish-rich sea. No contact, trade or war across water until a band invents boats; then empty islands can be settled.

    Who may work a field or tend a herd is set by the Fields & herds owned by rule: the whole camp, the lineage, the household (passed to an heir by the descent rule), or the ruler (who takes a quarter of the yield). Wealth raises status, and status wins wives.

    Each year

    1. Climate and shocks. A good, normal or bad year; rare forest fires, storms and epidemics.
    2. Forage, farm and herd. Workers pick plant, game or fish patches within range of camp by expected return. Hunting is high-variance. Patches deplete and regrow logistically, so carrying capacity emerges rather than being set. Camps move toward richer ground.
    3. Share. Food flows by the sharing gene (household, lineage, camp, or ruler with an elite cut), with some demand-sharing between groups. Pooling invites free-riding unless norms are enforced.
    4. Live and die. Forager-calibrated hazards (≈18% infant mortality, ≈60% reach 15) for infant illness, infection, accident, other illness and old age, amplified by hunger and reduced by medicine. Fathers, grandmothers and residence rules change child survival.
    5. Pair and give birth. Marriage rules, incest avoidance, polygyny by status, intermarriage across societies, birth spacing, maternal death, famine infanticide.
    6. Conflict. Raids decided by governance from hunger, grievance, unpaired men, relative strength and the leader's temperament. Captives are killed, ransomed, enslaved or adopted. A crushing win can conquer a weakened rival.
    7. Trade. Peaceful neighbours trade: a yield bonus, famine relief, technology diffusion — and disease.
    8. Know-how. Skill is per person and taught to children; a society's level is its best few practitioners. Fifteen technologies (including six medicines) are invented, diffused and lost when experts die.
    9. Culture evolves. Rare drift, imitation of a visibly more successful neighbour, conquest, and fission into daughter bands with a mutated genome.

    Sources the mechanics lean on

    • Read (2003), multi-agent !Kung demography — birth spacing and marriage rules set population.
    • JASSS (2017), minimum viable hunter-gatherer populations — restrictive marriage hurts small groups.
    • SFI working paper 2017-12-037, the polygyny puzzle; Francesconi, Ghiglino & Perry, an evolutionary theory of monogamy.
    • Turchin & Gavrilets; Turchin et al. (2013) — war as cultural multilevel selection.
    • Epstein & Axtell, Sugarscape; Janssen (2009), Artificial Anasazi — carrying capacity dominates.
    • Gurven & Kaplan (2007) life tables (approximate baselines; every parameter is in src/params.js).
